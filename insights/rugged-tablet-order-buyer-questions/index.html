What Buyers Ask Before a Rugged Tablet Order

Procurement questions · Minimum order · Sample and production timing · Certification and warranty

Home / News / Procurement Questions

By Xinhong Smart Sales Engineering · Published 9 November 2026

Quick answer: A first rugged tablet order usually turns on four numbers and two documents. The numbers are the minimum order of 2 units, a sample built in about 3 days, mass production about 25 days after sample approval, and a 12-month warranty that can be extended to 36 months on volume orders. The two documents are the specification sheet with exact module part numbers, and the certification status for your destination market. The rest of the conversation is a variation on those six things.

Most enquiries reach us with the same shape: a project, a quantity, a date, and a device in a brochure that may or may not still be available in six months. A buyer who has done this before asks a small number of questions and reads the answers carefully, because the difference between two suppliers rarely shows up in the unit price.

The questions below are the ones that come up on those calls, in roughly the order they are asked. The answers are the ones we give, including the parts that are inconvenient to put in a catalogue.

What the first email usually asks

A real one, shortened only by removing the project name: “We are bidding for a warehouse rollout in Q1. Need 300 units, Android, 2D scanning, IP65 or better, must arrive before February. What is your MOQ, sample lead time and price? Can you do 300 by January?”

Four questions are packed into that paragraph, and three of them have a published answer. The fourth, price, cannot have one until the module set and the destination market are fixed — that is the subject of a separate note on what moves a quotation.

Order size: why two units

Two units is the floor, and the reason is practical rather than commercial. One unit goes to the field trial, the other stays with your IT team for device enrolment and application testing, so the rollout does not stall while the first device is in someone else's hands. A standard build at two units is quoted like any other order; the unit price is simply the two-unit price, and it is higher than the 300-unit price for reasons that have nothing to do with negotiation.

Below two units the setup work dominates the cost. Above a few hundred, the constraints change from price to scheduling: whether the line has capacity in the month you need, and whether the radio bands for your market have already been verified on that model.

Sample and production: the dates that move

Three days to build a sample of an agreed standard configuration, then shipping. That figure assumes the configuration is fixed; a different radio band set, a larger battery or a new housing is scheduled separately and quoted before the sample is built.

Mass production runs about 25 days from sample approval for the configuration and quantity on the order. Two things extend it, and both are visible at quotation stage if you ask: certification for the destination market, and tooling for a custom housing. A supplier who quotes 25 days for a build that still needs a radio approval is quoting the easy half of the schedule.

If your date is fixed — a tender opening, a site handover — say so in the first message. A sample that arrives after the pilot start date is worth less than a plain answer about the date.

Certification: what is already covered

Three items are on the standard build and do not change the price: GMS certification on the models whose specification lists it, Ministry of Public Security certification on the capacitive fingerprint module, and the IP65 or IP67 rating the model is tested to in our own facility.

Everything else — CE, UKCA, FCC, or local type approval for the bands you need — is scoped per project, agreed before tooling and quoted as a line item. Retrofitting a radio approval means retesting the device, so it is cheaper to decide this early than to add it after the first shipment. The certification guide lists what each market asks for; certification support covers how the test plan is scheduled.

For anything described as already certified, ask for the certificate number. A buyer who checks that is not being difficult — a certificate that cannot be produced is a liability in a tender, not a detail.

Modules: ask for the part number

On most models the data capture modules are selectable. Scan engines come from Honeywell, Zebra or Newland depending on the model and the symbologies in scope; fingerprint capture can be capacitive or optical; RFID can be low frequency, high frequency or UHF. The rugged tablet range and the biometric tablet range list which options exist per model.

The XH-500 specification shows how that split is written down on a real page: standard modules first, optional ones after, each with the note that compatibility is confirmed at order stage. That layout is worth copying into your own comparison sheet.

What matters in a quotation is not the module family but the exact part number, the SDK, and the output format. Two modules from the same family can behave differently on the same firmware, and “fingerprint option” on a quotation is not a specification. Ask for the part number and the output format in writing, then compare those two lines between suppliers — that comparison is worth more than the price difference.

Warranty and the years after it

The standard term is 12 months on manufacturing defects, extendable to 36 months on volume orders. The scope is written into the quotation and the warranty card ships in the box, because the boundary between a defect and damage depends on how the device was used.

The question buyers ask next is what happens in year three. Every unit is serialised, so a failure can be traced to its production record, test data and module lot — which turns a repeat failure into something diagnosable rather than anecdotal. Repairs, spare parts and the warranty scope are listed separately in the quotation, so the cost of keeping a fleet alive after the warranty ends is visible before the order rather than after it. That total is usually the number that decides between two otherwise similar devices; the cost-of-ownership note works through it.

Shipping, forwarders and paperwork

Shipments leave Shenzhen under the incoterms agreed with the order, and collection by your own forwarder is a normal arrangement. What we need for that is the forwarder's address, the consignee details and the document set your market requires, because export documentation is prepared before packing rather than after. Carton and pallet specifications are agreed with the order as well; freight for a 300-unit consignment is not a parcel shipment with a bigger label.

Comparing two quotes that look the same

Two quotations can sit within a few dollars of each other and differ by a year of usable life. The lines worth comparing are the ones that are usually missing: the module part numbers, the band list for your market, the test report behind the IP rating, the Android version with its security patch commitment, and whether the acceptance criteria are written down at all. The RFP note sets out eight specifications worth writing into a tender and the acceptance test that makes each one enforceable; the buying guide covers the same ground for a fleet refresh rather than a tender.

What to put in the first email

Six items are enough to answer with a platform, a module set and a date:

  1. The workflow and what the device has to capture.
  2. The environment: indoor, vehicle, outdoor, freezer, dusty, wet.
  3. The modules in scope, with the standard or profile if there is one.
  4. The quantity, split between pilot and rollout if both exist.
  5. The destination market, because it decides the radio bands and certifications.
  6. The date units have to be on site, and whether that date is fixed.

With those six, the reply names a model, the module set and the lead time. Without them the honest answer is another question, which wastes a week.

What is the minimum order quantity for a rugged tablet?

Two units on a standard build. One is for the field trial and one for application and device-management testing, so the pilot and the rollout do not queue behind the same device.

How long does a sample take?

About three days to build an agreed standard configuration, plus shipping. A different radio band set, a battery change or a new housing is scheduled separately and quoted first.

Which certifications come with the standard build?

GMS on the models whose specification lists it, Ministry of Public Security certification on the capacitive fingerprint module, and the IP65 or IP67 rating the model is tested to. CE, UKCA, FCC and local radio approvals are scoped and quoted per project.

What does the warranty cover?

Twelve months on manufacturing defects as standard, up to 36 months on volume orders. The scope is written into the quotation and the warranty card ships with the unit.

Can I use my own forwarder in Shenzhen?

Yes. Shipments leave Shenzhen under the incoterms agreed with the order; the forwarder's address, consignee details and the document set for your market are needed before packing.

Do I have to pay for tooling?

Only when the housing changes. An OEM build keeps the existing housing and changes the module set, branding and packaging; an ODM build adds mechanical design and tooling for a new housing, which carries its own cost and schedule.

Send the six lines

If you are at the quotation stage, the six items above are enough to start. If the project is still being scoped, the same six lines still get a useful reply — usually a question about the environment, which is where most specification mistakes begin.
