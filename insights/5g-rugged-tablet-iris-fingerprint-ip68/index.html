5G Rugged Tablet: Iris, Fingerprint and IP68 Sealing

Radio and sealing · Capture modules · Antenna and aperture conflicts · What to put in the RFQ

Home / News / 5G and Identity Capture

By Xinhong Smart Engineering Team · Published 23 November 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer: 5G, IP68 and iris capture pull the industrial design in three different directions, and most shipping platforms satisfy two of the three rather than all of them. A five-antenna 5G radio needs space and dissipates heat; IP68 sealing forbids the gasketed doors and hot-swap batteries that field teams ask for; an iris camera needs a window in the front bezel, which is where drops land. The combination is achievable, but as a build decision rather than a catalogue item - which is why the specification has to state which requirement wins when they conflict.

What each requirement actually costs

Each line in a tender looks independent. In the housing, they are not: every one of them takes volume, power or a hole.

Where the requirements collide

Three conflicts come up in almost every programme that asks for this combination.

5G heat against IP68 sealing

A sealed enclosure has no vent, so heat leaves through the housing surface. A 5G modem running at a weak signal is the hottest component in the device. An IP67 design that runs warm becomes an IP68 design that runs hotter, because the tighter sealing usually means a thicker wall and less internal airflow. If the deployment has the device on charge in a vehicle in summer, ask for the thermal test with the radio transmitting, not the standby number.

IP68 against the battery and SIM tray

The trade-off is direct: every door that opens is a seal that will be opened thousands of times in the field. IP68 builds usually seal the battery inside the chassis and move the SIM behind a gasketed tray, which means a swap takes minutes and a tool rather than seconds. If the shift pattern needs a mid-shift battery change, the sealing target has to be argued down, or the charging design has to remove the need for a swap.

Iris window against drop survival

An iris camera needs an optical path in the front bezel. That window is a hard, flat, brittle element in the one place a dropped device lands. It also has to stay clean: a scratched window degrades the capture long before the device fails. The engineering answer is a recessed window with a replaceable cover, not a flush lens, and an acceptance test that includes capture quality after the drop test rather than instead of it.

Why programmes ask for all four

The combination usually comes from a single requirement: verify a person, in the field, on a connection you do not control, and leave an audit trail. Fingerprint covers most populations; the card reader handles the credential the citizen already carries; iris covers the cases where fingers are worn, bandaged or missing, and where a contact capture is unacceptable for hygiene reasons. 5G comes from the wish to avoid a local server, and IP68 from the weather and the wash-down.

Each of those is defensible on its own. The problem is that they are usually written into the tender by four different people, and nobody assigns the priority order. That order is the single most useful thing a buyer can send.

What to put in the RFQ

  1. A priority order for the five requirements, so the supplier knows what to trade when they conflict.
  2. The capture standards, not the module names: fingerprint record format and FAP profile, card standards, iris standards if the registry names one.
  3. Offline behaviour: what has to work with no network, for how long, and what has to be synced later.
  4. The sealing target with its test condition: depth and duration for IP68, and whether the device has to be serviceable in the field.
  5. The thermal environment: in-vehicle charging, direct sun, ambient temperature, and whether the radio transmits continuously.
  6. The bands for each destination market, and whether roaming across borders is in scope.
  7. Certification ownership: who runs the type approval, who pays, and which market's requirements are in scope.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can one rugged tablet have 5G, IP68 and iris recognition together?
Yes, as an OEM or ODM build, but rarely as an off-the-shelf model. The three requirements compete for the same housing: 5G needs antenna volume and heat path, IP68 forbids serviceable doors, and iris needs a window in the front bezel where drops land. A supplier who quotes all three in a catalogue item should be able to show the sealing test report and the thermal test with the radio transmitting.
Is IP68 worth it over IP67 for a 5G tablet?
Only if the deployment actually immerses the device. IP67 covers 30 minutes at one metre, which handles rain, wash-down and a drop into standing water. IP68 is continuous immersion at a depth agreed with the maker, and it usually costs a serviceable battery door. If the device is carried rather than dunked, IP67 with a sealed battery is the better trade.
Does iris recognition work through glasses?
Partly. Modern iris cameras handle clear spectacles at the specified working distance, but reflections, tinted lenses and progressive lenses all reduce capture quality. If a large share of the population wears glasses, the acceptance test has to include them, and the protocol needs a documented fallback to fingerprint or card rather than a failed enrolment.
What working distance should I specify for iris capture?
Roughly 30 to 50 cm, and the number matters more than it sounds: it sets the illuminator power, the lens and the operator's posture. A short distance is easier to light but feels intrusive; a long one is comfortable but needs a stronger infrared source and a steadier hold. Specify the distance and the acceptable capture time together, because they trade against each other.
Does 5G reduce battery life on a rugged tablet?
Yes, particularly at the edge of coverage, where the modem transmits at higher power for longer. The practical consequence is that a runtime figure measured on LTE does not transfer to a 5G deployment. Ask for the runtime with the 5G radio attached and at a realistic signal level, and plan a charging point or a spare pack for shifts that run the radio continuously.
Which is more reliable in the field, fingerprint or iris?
Fingerprint is faster and cheaper, and it fails on worn, wet or dirty hands, which is common in manual work. Iris needs no contact and tolerates gloves, but it needs a steady hold, a clean window and cooperation. Most identity programmes end up with both, fingerprint as the default and iris as the exception path, which is why the requirement usually arrives as "and iris as an option".
