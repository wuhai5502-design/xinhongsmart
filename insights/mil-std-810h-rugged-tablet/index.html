MIL-STD-810H Explained: What "Military Grade" Really Means

Test Method · Drop 516.8 · Vibration 514.8 · Thermal · How to Read a Real Report

Home / News / MIL-STD-810H Explained: What "Military Grade" Really Means

"Military grade" is one of the most overused phrases in the rugged device industry. This guide explains what MIL-STD-810H actually is, which methods matter for industrial buyers, and — most importantly — how to tell a real test from a marketing claim.

It is the companion piece to our IP ratings guide: IP covers dust and water, MIL-STD covers environmental and mechanical stress. You need both.

What MIL-STD-810H Actually Is

MIL-STD-810 is a United States Department of Defense test method standard. The "H" denotes the current revision. Critically:

MIL-STD-810H is a set of test procedures, not a certification. There is no issuing authority, no certificate, and no pass/fail threshold defined by the standard itself — the test plan defines the conditions and the acceptance criteria.

This is why you should be cautious when a vendor claims "MIL-STD-810H certified". The honest phrasing is "tested to MIL-STD-810H Method X".

The Methods That Matter for Rugged Tablets

MIL-STD-810H contains dozens of methods. These are the ones relevant to industrial mobile devices:

MethodStressWhy it matters
516.8 Procedure IVShock / transit dropHandheld drops, falls from carts and vehicles
514.8VibrationForklift and vehicle mounting, continuous engine vibration
501.7High temperatureSummer outdoor use, hot vehicles, near ovens or kilns
502.7Low temperatureCold chain, freezers, winter outdoor work
503.7Temperature shockMoving between freezer and ambient, or outdoors into heated buildings
507.6HumidityTropical climates, condensation, food processing
509.7Salt fogMarine, ports, coastal and offshore deployments
510.7Sand and dustConstruction, mining, deserts, bulk material handling
505.7Solar radiationPermanently outdoor kiosks and yard operations
500.6Low pressureHigh-altitude sites and air freight

The one everyone quotes: Method 516.8 drop

When a datasheet says "MIL-STD-810H drop tested", it almost always means Method 516.8, Procedure IV (transit drop). But the standard does not fix a single height — the test plan does. You must ask:

  • Drop height — commonly 1.2 m or 1.5 m
  • Number of drops — commonly 26 (all faces, edges, corners)
  • Surface — concrete, steel, or plywood (dramatically different severity)
  • State — operating (powered on) or non-operating
  • Acceptance criteria — "functional after test" vs "no visible damage"

A "1.2 m onto plywood" result and a "1.5 m onto concrete, operating" result are very different claims, yet both can be described as MIL-STD-810H drop tested.

MIL-STD vs IP vs IK: Three Different Questions

StandardMeasuresQuestion it answers
IP (IEC 60529)Dust and water ingress"Will dust or water get inside?"
IK (IEC 62262)Impact energy (joules)"Will a localised impact break it?"
MIL-STD-810HEnvironmental & mechanical stress"Will it survive drops, vibration, heat, cold, humidity?"

A device can be IP67 and still crack when dropped — ingress protection says nothing about impact. Conversely, a device may pass drop testing with an IP65 enclosure. Always specify all three.

How to Spot a Misleading Claim

  1. "Certified" — there is no MIL-STD-810 certification. Red flag.
  2. No method numbers — "MIL-STD-810 compliant" without specific methods is unverifiable.
  3. Only one or two methods — a vendor may test only drop and vibration, then claim broad compliance.
  4. No third-party report — in-house results cannot be validated. Ask for the lab name and report number.
  5. Sample ≠ production unit — confirm in writing that the tested unit matches the shipping configuration.
  6. Vague acceptance criteria — "functional after test" can permit a cracked screen.

How to Specify It Properly

Copy this language into your RFQ or purchase order:

Device shall be tested to MIL-STD-810H:
- Method 516.8 Proc. IV: 1.5 m, 26 drops, concrete, operating
- Method 514.8: vibration profile per category [X]
- Method 501.7 / 502.7: operating -20 °C to +60 °C
- Method 507.6: humidity
- Method 509.7: salt fog (if marine)
Evidence: third-party laboratory report (number + date)
on the exact shipping configuration.

Specifying like this makes the requirement enforceable. Vague phrases like "military grade" are not.

Practical Recommendation

  • Most industrial deployments: 516.8 drop (1.5 m) + 514.8 vibration + 501.7/502.7 temperature + IP67 + IK07/IK08.
  • Add 509.7 salt fog for marine, port or coastal sites.
  • Add 510.7 dust for mining, construction and bulk handling.
  • Only pay for tests your environment actually needs — unnecessary testing inflates cost without benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is MIL-STD-810H a certification?
No — this is the most common misconception. MIL-STD-810H is a US Department of Defense test method standard: it defines how to test, not who certifies. There is no issuing body and no certificate. Vendors may say "tested to MIL-STD-810H"; if they claim "MIL-STD-810H certified", treat it as a red flag.
What drop height does MIL-STD-810H specify?
It does not specify a single height. Method 516.8 Procedure IV (transit drop) drops the unit onto a specified surface from a height defined by the test plan — commonly 1.2 m or 1.5 m, typically 26 drops across all faces, edges and corners. Always ask: what height, how many drops, onto what surface, operating or non-operating?
What is the difference between MIL-STD-810 and IP ratings?
They measure completely different things. IP (IEC 60529) measures ingress of dust and water. MIL-STD-810H covers environmental stresses including drop/shock, vibration, temperature, humidity, salt fog and solar radiation. A device can be IP67 and still fail a drop test — you generally need both.
Does a MIL-STD-810H claim mean the screen will not crack?
No. Method 516.8 tests whether the unit remains functional after the specified drops; cosmetic damage and even a cracked cover glass can still pass, depending on the acceptance criteria in the test plan. Ask what the pass criteria were — "functional after test" is not the same as "undamaged".
How many methods were tested? Why does it matter?
MIL-STD-810H contains dozens of methods. A vendor may test only two or three (often just drop and vibration) and still say "MIL-STD-810 tested". Ask for the specific list of methods tested. For genuine industrial use you typically want drop, vibration, high/low temperature, humidity, and often salt fog or dust.
Who performed the test — the vendor or an independent lab?
This matters greatly. Independent third-party laboratory reports (SGS, TUV, Intertek and similar) carry a report number, date and accredited method. In-house testing is not worthless, but it is unverifiable. Always request the actual report, not a datasheet icon.
Was the tested unit the same as what I will receive?
A frequent gap: engineering samples are sometimes built differently from production units. Ask the vendor to confirm in writing that the tested configuration matches the shipping configuration, including battery, cover glass and any accessory fitted during testing.
What is IK rating and how does it relate?
IK (IEC 62262) rates resistance to mechanical impact energy in joules, from IK00 to IK10. It complements MIL-STD-810H: IK measures a defined pendulum impact, while 516.8 simulates real transit drops. For industrial tablets ask for IK07 minimum, IK08 preferred, in addition to drop testing.
Do I need salt fog or solar radiation testing?
Only if your environment demands it. Salt fog (Method 509.7) matters for marine, port and coastal deployments; solar radiation (Method 505.7) matters for permanently outdoor installations. Specifying tests you do not need adds cost without benefit.
How should I write MIL-STD requirements into a purchase order?
Be specific: "Tested to MIL-STD-810H Method 516.8 Procedure IV, 1.5 m, 26 drops onto concrete, operating, per third-party report number X dated Y, on shipping configuration." Vague phrases like "military grade" or "MIL-STD compliant" are unenforceable.
