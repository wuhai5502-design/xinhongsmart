Civil Identity Enrolment in Zimbabwe: How the XH-500 Is Configured

National ID enrolment · XH-500 10.1-inch tablet · Offline capture · Optional fingerprint and ID modules · 10,000 mAh

Home / News / Civil Identity Enrolment

By Xinhong Smart Engineering Team · Published 28 October 2026

Quick answer: National identity enrolment succeeds or fails on power and offline capacity rather than processing speed. A tablet with a 10,000 mAh pack, enough local storage for a full day of records, a module that captures the required biometric or document data, and an operating range wide enough for the field conditions is what keeps a rural enrolment drive running.

A national identity programme in Zimbabwe puts the XH-500 rugged tablet into the hands of enrolment teams working away from reliable power and coverage. That combination reframes the specification: the interesting questions are not processing power and screen size, but how long a unit lasts between charges, what happens when there is no signal, and whether the record it produces still reads ten years from now.

What follows is how we configure the device for work like this, and why each line matters more than it looks on a datasheet.

What Field Enrolment Demands

  • Offline-first capture. Most rural enrolment happens without usable data. Records have to queue locally, stay tamper-evident, and reconcile cleanly once uploaded.
  • A real power budget. The device has to finish a day's work, then wait for whatever charging source exists that evening.
  • Daylight readability. Gloss, direct sun and dust decide whether an operator can see the form.
  • A capture path that produces the required record. That means the right module, not the most features.
  • Records that outlive the device. Standardised output formats rather than a proprietary blob only one application can open.

The last point drives the earlier decisions. We set out the same argument for national programmes in Civil Identity Enrolment With a Biometric Tablet, including deduplication and the ten-year supply view.

Power Is the Constraint Nobody Budgets For

Enrolment is capture-heavy work: screen on at high brightness for hours, radios active, camera and biometric modules in constant use. Size the battery from the duty cycle, add margin for two years of ageing, then plan the charging route, because assuming access to mains power along a rural route is how fleets stall. Cradles and spare packs ship with units or as a separate line, and the XH-500 takes DC 5 V / 3 A through a 3.5 mm jack, which suits a vehicle supply.

Temperature matters as much as capacity. A lithium pack charged hot ages fast, so the -20 to +60 degrees Celsius operating range only helps if the devices are not left charging on a dashboard in the middle of the day.

Choosing Capture Modules by Record Requirement

Start from the record, not the module. If the register requires a two-finger slap or a ten-print card, the platen size class decides it, which is explained in FAP 10, FAP 20 and FAP 30. If the register requires a document scan or a captured face image checked for liveness, that is a different line on the build and a different test before acceptance.

The same applies to the biometric camera options. Iris and binocular liveness modules serve different record requirements and different throughput expectations, and neither is a substitute for deciding what the register actually has to hold.

Confirm Before Shipment

  • Carrier bands. Confirm the LTE bands against the operators you enrol with; if units move between regions, check both.
  • Ruggedness for the actual route. The listed drop condition is 1.2 m onto a wooden floor with the unit powered on. If your route is harder than that, say so before tooling rather than after delivery.
  • Import documentation. Whatever your market needs with the shipment, it is scoped before production.
  • Trial units. Sample quantity is two, built in about three days. Run them for a full enrolment day before committing to volume.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which modules does the XH-500 carry for identity enrolment?

One or more of: a capacitive or optical fingerprint module, an ID card recognition module, a binocular liveness or iris camera module, and LF, HF or UHF RFID. Each is selected at order stage and each needs its own confirmation of output format and SDK behaviour, so decide which the record requires before the sample is built.

Can the tablet enrol where there is no mobile coverage?

Yes. Enrolment records are stored on the device and uploaded when connectivity returns, which for most national programmes is the normal case rather than the exception. The offline queue, the record integrity checks and the reconciliation process belong to your application and should be tested with the intended daily volume before rollout.

How much battery does a day of field enrolment need?

Budget from your duty cycle rather than the quoted capacity. The XH-500 carries a built-in 10,000 mAh pack, but continuous capture, radios and screen time at high brightness consume far more than idle standby. Plan a charging strategy too: cradles or spare packs for the vehicle or generator you travel with, since assume nothing about grid access along the route.

Which radio bands should be confirmed before shipment?

Check the LTE bands against the carriers in the regions you enrol, particularly if units move between operators. The build also carries dual-band WiFi, Bluetooth 5.0 LE, GPS, BeiDou and GLONASS, but treat no band as covered until it is confirmed against your own operator list.

What should be decided before the order rather than after?

The record format, because it determines which module can be used; whether the enclosure needs a higher ingress rating for your conditions; whether your market requires conformance documentation with the shipment; and how the fleet will be maintained once units are spread across districts. Sample quantity is two units and each of those questions is cheaper to settle on a real device.
