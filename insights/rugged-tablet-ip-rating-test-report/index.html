How to Read an IP67 Test Report for Rugged Tablets

IEC 60529 · Dust and immersion · Sample state · What a real report contains

Home / News / IP Test Reports

By Xinhong Smart Engineering Team · Published 20 November 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer: IP67 means the enclosure kept dust out entirely and survived 30 minutes at one metre of water under IEC 60529. The rating says nothing about how it was tested, on which unit, with which ports closed, or whether the sample was a prototype or a production build. A report answers those questions; a datasheet does not.

What IEC 60529 actually measures

The code has two digits and no meaning beyond them. The first is solids, the second is water. Neither digit implies anything about the other, which is why "IP68" on a device tells you nothing about its dust behaviour unless the first digit is stated too.

CodeWhat is actually appliedWhere it comes from
IP5XTalc chamber, typically eight hours; some dust ingress allowed if it does not affect operationDusty warehouses, grain and cement
IP6XSame chamber; no dust ingress at allMining, construction, food processing
IPX56.3 mm nozzle, 12.5 litres per minute, from all directionsHose-down, general wash
IPX612.5 mm nozzle, 100 litres per minuteDeck wash, heavy spray
IPX7Immersion at 1 m for 30 minutesDrop into a tank, standing water
IPX8Continuous immersion, depth and duration agreed between buyer and makerMarine, sub-surface work

Note the asymmetry in that table. IPX7 names its conditions — one metre, thirty minutes. IPX8 does not, which is why an IP68 claim without a stated depth is not comparable to anything. Two IP68 devices may have been tested at one metre and at fifty, and both are correct.

The four conditions that decide whether a claim holds

Given the same "IP67" on two datasheets, the difference in real performance usually comes down to four things that the code never mentions.

1. Which edition of the standard. IEC 60529 has been revised several times. Older reports cite older editions, and a claim tested to a 1990s edition is not evidence for a current tender that names the 2013 one.

2. The state of the unit when it went in. A device tested with its SIM tray sealed, every port door shut and no cable attached is a different device from the one your operator carries. The report should say which ports were open, how they were protected, and whether the device was powered during the test.

3. Whether the sample was a prototype or a production unit. Hand-built prototypes routinely outperform production units, because an engineer sealed them by hand. A report on a prototype says the design can pass; a report on a serial-numbered production unit says the line can.

4. What counted as a pass. This is the most quietly important one. Some reports accept "no water in the enclosure". Others accept "water present but no effect on function". Those are different products in the field. The verdict wording matters more than the number in the title.

ConditionWhat it changesWhat to look for in the report
Standard editionWhich test conditions were actually appliedThe edition number, not just "IEC 60529"
Unit stateWhether the tested device matches the one shippedPorts open or closed, tray fitted, battery in place
Sample typeDesign capability versus production capabilityA serial or build identifier on a production unit
Pass criterionHow much ingress the supplier acceptsThe verdict wording: no ingress, or ingress with no functional effect

What a real IP67 report contains

A usable report is short. If any of the following is missing, the claim is a marketing statement rather than a test result.

  1. Laboratory name and accreditation number. An in-house summary is not a third-party report. Either can be legitimate, but the buyer should know which one they are reading.
  2. Report number and date. So it can be traced and so you can tell whether it predates a hardware revision.
  3. Standard and edition. "IEC 60529:1989+AMD1:1999" is a version; "IP67 compliant" is not.
  4. Sample identification. Model, and ideally a serial or build identifier, plus a note on whether it was a prototype.
  5. Test parameters. Depth and duration for the water test; chamber duration for the dust test.
  6. The configuration tested. Ports open or closed, doors fitted, battery installed, any seals removed for the test.
  7. Photographs of the sample before and after, and of any ingress found.
  8. A verdict per characteristic numeral — dust and water separately, not one combined "passed".

The traps that survive a careful reading

IPX7 is not IP67

An "IPX7" rating leaves the first digit unspecified: dust was not tested at all. Devices marketed as waterproof often carry IPX7 alone, and in a dusty warehouse that is the digit that matters.

IP67 is a point test, not a durability claim

The gasket that seals a unit on day one is compressed by every opening. The standard tests a representative sample; it does not promise that the ten-thousandth opening behaves like the first. This is why field failures cluster around SIM trays and port doors rather than around the housing itself.

Water tests and drop tests interact

A unit that passes IP67 and then passes a drop test has been tested twice, not tested in combination. The interesting question — does it still seal after the drop — is a different test, and most reports do not answer it. If your deployment involves drops onto wet floors, ask whether the sequence was run in that order.

IP69K is not simply "better"

IP69K is high-pressure, high-temperature jet cleaning. It says nothing about continuous immersion, and it is not a dust rating. Food and pharmaceutical plants want it; a logistics fleet usually does not, and paying for it buys nothing.

How the rating sits beside MIL-STD and IK

Three different questions, three different standards. IP is enclosure sealing. MIL-STD-810 covers environmental stress — heat, cold, vibration, drop — with each method carrying its own parameters. IK is impact resistance on the glass. A tender that asks for "rugged" and accepts a single IP rating has asked for a third of what it needs.

The MIL-STD explainer covers that side; the difference between the common IP levels is set out in IP65 vs IP67 vs IP68.

What to ask a supplier

  1. Can I see the report, not the datasheet? A PDF with a report number, not a line in a brochure.
  2. Which edition, and on which unit? Production serial beats prototype.
  3. What was the port configuration? And would it differ in the unit you ship me?
  4. What was the pass criterion? No ingress, or ingress without functional effect?
  5. Was the water test run before or after the drop test? Order changes what the result means.
  6. What is the service interval for the gaskets? If there is no answer, the rating has an expiry date nobody has told you about.

Why we send the report rather than the number

A datasheet line costs nothing to write and nothing to check. A report has a laboratory, a date, a serial number and a fail column — and it is the only version of the claim a buyer can verify.

When a customer asks whether a unit is IP67, the useful reply is the report that says so, with the parameters visible. If a supplier will not send it to a qualified buyer under NDA, that is itself information.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is IP67 waterproof?
IP67 means the enclosure is dust tight and survived 30 minutes at one metre of water. That covers a drop into a puddle, a wash-down, or rain driven by wind. It does not cover swimming, pressure washing at close range, or repeated immersion. For anything beyond a metre, the correct rating is IP68, and the depth and duration have to be stated by the maker because the standard does not fix them.
What is the difference between IP67 and IP68?
Both require the dust-tight first digit. The difference is the water test: IP67 is a fixed 1 metre for 30 minutes, while IP68 is continuous immersion at a depth and duration agreed between buyer and maker. An IP68 claim without a stated depth cannot be compared with another IP68 claim. In practice a well-built IP67 device often exceeds the IP67 conditions, but that is not what the rating certifies.
Do I need the test report or is the datasheet enough?
For a tender, the report. The datasheet states an intent; the report states what was done, to which unit, under which edition of the standard, and with which ports sealed. If a supplier cannot produce a report, the claim is unverified — which may still be acceptable for a small order but should not be written into a contract as a specification.
Does the IP rating survive a drop?
Not automatically. The standard tests sealing and impact separately. A unit can pass IP67 and pass a drop test and still leak after a drop, because the two were never tested in sequence. If your site involves drops onto wet floors, ask explicitly whether the sequence was run, and expect most suppliers to say no.
How long do the gaskets last?
It depends on how often the unit is opened, what it is cleaned with and how it is stored. Silicone gaskets tolerate heat and cleaning agents better than cheaper elastomers, and a gasket compressed thousands of times takes a permanent set. Ask for the service interval and for the replacement part; a sealed unit with no gasket service path has a finite field life that no datasheet mentions.
Is IP69K better than IP67?
They answer different questions. IP69K is protection against high-pressure, high-temperature jet cleaning, which matters in food and pharmaceutical plants. It says nothing about immersion and nothing about dust. For a logistics or field-service deployment, IP67 with a dust-tight first digit is the more relevant specification, and IP69K adds cost without adding protection where it is needed.
