What FAP 10, FAP 20 and FAP 30 Mean on a Fingerprint Tablet

Three short codes decide whether a fingerprint taken on a tablet can be checked by an FBI system, stored in an ISO-format database, or only by the app that captured it. Here is what each one covers, in millimetres and pixels, and how to choose between them.

The three profiles, in numbers

FAP stands for Fingerprint Acquisition Profile, and the codes come from Appendix F of the US FBI's fingerprint capture specification. Each profile fixes the size of the platen, the number of fingers captured in one placement, and the pixel dimensions of the resulting image at 500 ppi.

ProfileFingersPlatenImage at 500 ppiTypical job
FAP 10One1.0 × 1.0 in (25.4 × 25.4 mm)256 × 360 pxVerification at a counter
FAP 20Two1.6 × 1.5 in (40.6 × 38.1 mm)300 × 400 pxTwo-finger enrolment
FAP 30Four (slap)3.2 × 3.0 in (81.3 × 76.2 mm)400 × 500 pxTen-print enrolment, border and police work

Sampling rate and grey-scale depth stay the same across all three: 500 ppi, 8-bit, 256 grey levels. What changes is the platen area, and therefore the size of the device that has to carry it.

Why the platen decides the form factor

A FAP 30 window is close to the size of a credit card. A 5-inch handheld can host it, but only as a module bolted onto the body, and the grip suffers. A 10.1-inch biometric tablet has the bezel for it, plus room for the battery that a large platen and a bright screen draw from. That is why four-finger capture usually ends up on a tablet rather than a handheld.

The XH-400 takes that route. It is a 10.1-inch biometric tablet with the fingerprint module offered in FAP 10, FAP 20 or FAP 30, alongside IP65 sealing, a 10000 mAh battery and a wired RJ45 port for benches where Wi-Fi is congested. Where the job is single-finger verification on the move, the 5-inch XH-100 covers the same three profiles in a body you can hold in one hand.

FBI and ISO solve different problems

The two standards get quoted together so often that they start to sound like the same requirement. They are not.

FBI Appendix F governs image quality: geometry, 500 ppi sampling, grey-scale depth, and how much distortion the platen may introduce. FIPS 201 governs the credential side, when the finger is tied to a PIV card. EBTS and the WSQ compression format move the image into US systems.

ISO/IEC governs the record rather than the glass. ISO/IEC 19794-2 defines the minutiae template, 19794-4 the image record, and 30107-3 how the sensor should behave when something that is not a finger is put on the platen.

The consequence is practical rather than academic. A sensor can pass an FBI image-quality test and still fail an integration, because the back-end expects an ISO record and receives something else. If the fingerprint leaves the device — an AFIS, a national ID system, a bank's onboarding platform — confirm the record format before you confirm the sensor.

Picking a profile by workflow

Counter verification. A customer or citizen places one finger to confirm who they are. FAP 10. Anything larger adds cost without adding throughput.

Account opening with enrolment. Two fingers captured together, stored as the reference record. FAP 20.

Ten-print enrolment. Two four-finger slaps plus the thumbs make up a ten-print record, and FAP 30 is the profile that captures the slap in one placement instead of four.

Inspection while walking. One hand holds the device, so the platen has to stay small. A handheld with FAP 10 does more for the workflow than a tablet that needs a table.

Most enquiries for a biometric tablet ask for FAP 30, because it reads as the most capable option. Roughly half of those deployments end up capturing one or two fingers, which FAP 10 or FAP 20 would have handled at a lower unit cost.

Six things to confirm before you order

1. The profile your workflow actually needs — not the largest one on the list.

2. The record format your back-end ingests — WSQ for FBI-side systems, ISO/IEC 19794-4 for most others, or a vendor template if match stays on the device.

3. Where templates live — on-device matching, or server-side storage with the device acting as a capture terminal.

4. Real rejection rates — for the fingers you will actually meet: dry, dusty, calloused, or wet. Ask for the number, not the marketing line.

5. The enclosure — IP rating, and how the platen gets cleaned when the device works in dust or gets wiped down.

6. The test report itself — Appendix F, FIPS 201, ISO — rather than a logo on a slide.

Questions we get asked

Is FAP 30 always the better choice for a fingerprint tablet?
No. FAP 30 needs a platen of about 81 x 76 mm, which pushes the device to tablet size and raises the cost. If the workflow verifies one finger or enrols two, FAP 10 or FAP 20 does the same job on a smaller, cheaper device. FAP 30 earns its place in ten-print enrolment, where a four-finger slap replaces four separate placements.
Does one device need both FBI and ISO support?
It depends on where the fingerprint goes. If the image never leaves the device and your own application makes the match, image quality is the main concern. If the record is sent to an AFIS, a national ID system or a bank platform, that back-end will expect a specific format, usually WSQ for FBI workflows or an ISO/IEC 19794 record. Confirm the format before confirming the sensor.
