What Certifications Does a Rugged Tablet Need? IP, MIL-STD-810H, IK, CE, FCC and ATEX Explained

IEC 60529 · MIL-STD-810H · IK Impact · CE / UKCA / FCC · UN 38.3 · ATEX & IECEx · How to Verify a Claim

Home / News / What Certifications Does a Rugged Tablet Need?

"Rugged" is not a standard. Any vendor can print the word on a datasheet. What makes a device genuinely survive a warehouse, a construction site or an offshore platform is a set of independently tested specifications — and knowing which ones actually apply to your environment. This guide maps the four families of certification, flags the ones that are legally mandatory versus optional, and shows how to tell a real test report from a marketing icon. It pairs with our IP rating comparison and our MIL-STD-810H breakdown.

The one rule: certifications are bought for a risk, not for a datasheet. Write down the top three ways your current devices fail, then specify only the standards that address them.

The Four Families of Rugged Certification

Most confusion comes from mixing these up. They are issued by different bodies, prove different things, and none of them substitutes for another.

Family 1 — Ingress Protection (IEC 60529)

The IP code covers only two hazards: solid particles and liquids. First digit is solids (5 = dust-protected, 6 = dust-tight), second is liquids (5 = low-pressure jets, 6 = powerful jets, 7 = 1 m immersion for 30 minutes, 8 = immersion to manufacturer-stated conditions, 9K = high-pressure hot steam).

For industrial duty require first digit 6 and, for outdoor or washdown fleets, IP67 as the practical baseline. Full detail in IP65 vs IP67 vs IP68 vs IP69K Explained.

Family 2 — Environmental and Mechanical (MIL-STD-810)

MIL-STD-810H is a set of test methods published by the US Department of Defense. It is not a certificate and there is no issuing authority — a vendor either ran the methods and can show the report, or did not. The methods that matter for a tablet:

MethodWhat it simulatesWho actually needs it
516.8 ShockDrops and handling impactsEveryone — this is the core "rugged" claim
514.8 VibrationVehicle and machinery mountingForklifts, trucks, rail, plant equipment
501.7 / 502.7 High / low temperatureOperating and storage extremesDesert sites, cold chain, unconditioned vehicles
507.6 HumidityTropical and condensing environmentsCoastal Asia, food processing, greenhouses
509.7 Salt fogCorrosionMarine, offshore, ports, road de-icing fleets
505.7 Solar radiationSunload and UVContinuous outdoor use

Ask which methods were run, at what severity, and on which sample. "Compliant with MIL-STD-810H" without method numbers is meaningless.

Family 3 — Mechanical Impact (IK, IEC 62262)

The IK code rates resistance to a defined impact energy in joules, from IK00 to IK10 (20 J). It is the missing half of the IP story: a dust-tight, immersion-proof tablet can still shatter when a tool lands on it. Pair it with the drop method above.

Family 4 — Market Access and Legal Compliance

These are not about ruggedness. They are the minimum legal permission to import, sell and operate the device in a country, and they apply to every tablet including consumer models.

Two practical traps. First, radio compliance is per-configuration: a tablet certified with one cellular module is not automatically certified with another. Second, UN 38.3 is a shipping requirement — without it a carrier can refuse your air freight regardless of product quality.

Industry-Specific Schemes You May Also Need

  • ATEX (EU) / IECEx (international) — mandatory where flammable gas, vapour or dust is present. Most industrial tablet deployments sit at Zone 2 / Category 3; Zone 1 and Zone 0 are progressively stricter and considerably more expensive.
  • IEC 60601-1 — medical electrical equipment safety, required when the device is used in patient care environments. Also expect disinfectant compatibility testing.
  • E-Mark (ECE R10) and ISO 7637-2 — electromagnetic compatibility and electrical transients for permanently mounted in-vehicle equipment.
  • IP69K (ISO 20653) — high-pressure, high-temperature washdown for food, beverage and pharmaceutical plants.
  • FIPS 140-3 / Common Criteria — validated cryptography and security evaluation, usually demanded by government and defence buyers rather than commercial fleets.
  • ISO 9001 — worth noting separately: this certifies a manufacturer's quality system, not a product. It says nothing about a specific tablet.

What Certifications Do NOT Tell You

This is where procurement decisions go wrong. A wall of certificates can still hide a device that fails in twelve months.

  1. Battery cycle life. No ruggedness standard requires a vendor to state how many charge cycles the pack survives. Ask for the figure at 80% retained capacity.
  2. Repairability and spare parts availability. Not certified anywhere. Ask how long spares are guaranteed.
  3. Software and security update policy. Android version and patch cadence are commercial commitments, not certifications.
  4. Screen behaviour. Sunlight readability in nits, glove touch, wet-finger touch and stylus accuracy are separate specifications.
  5. Real mixed-stress survival. Standards test one stress at a time. A hot tablet dropped into cold water is not in any standard.
  6. Accessory durability. Docks, chargers and hand straps frequently fail before the tablet does, and are rarely covered by the device report.

How to Verify a Vendor's Certification Claim

  1. Request the full test report PDF — never accept a certificate graphic or a datasheet icon.
  2. Confirm the laboratory is a recognised third party (SGS, TUV, Intertek, or an accredited national lab) and note the report number.
  3. Check the standard and version: IEC 60529, MIL-STD-810H with method numbers, IEC 62262, and so on.
  4. Verify the tested sample matches your configuration — housing, ports and covers, battery, display, dock.
  5. Check the date and hardware revision. A report for an older build does not cover a revised one.
  6. For large orders, cross-check the report number with the issuing laboratory.
  7. Get the commitments written into the purchase order: standard, conditions, and whether accessories were included in testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is MIL-STD-810H a certification?
No. MIL-STD-810 is a United States Department of Defense test method standard, not a certification, and no government body issues a MIL-STD certificate. A vendor either tests to the methods and publishes the results, or does not. Always ask for the actual test report with method numbers, laboratory name and dates.
Do I need both an IP rating and MIL-STD-810H?
Yes, because they test different things. The IP code covers dust and water ingress only. MIL-STD-810H covers drop, vibration, temperature extremes, humidity and salt fog. Neither covers mechanical impact from a concentrated blow, which is why the IK rating is a useful third data point.
Is the CE mark enough to sell a rugged tablet in Europe?
CE is the minimum legal requirement, not a quality mark. For a rugged tablet with wireless radios it normally means conformity with the Radio Equipment Directive 2014/53/EU, plus EMC, low-voltage safety, RoHS and, where applicable, WEEE registration. ATEX is a separate conformity route if the device is used in explosive atmospheres.
What is ATEX Zone 2 and do I need it?
ATEX Zone 2 covers areas where an explosive atmosphere is unlikely in normal operation and, if it occurs, lasts only briefly. Most industrial tablets for oil and gas, paint shops, grain handling and chemical plants are specified for Zone 2 / Category 3 equipment. Zone 1 and Zone 0 are progressively stricter and far more expensive. Confirm your zone classification with your safety engineer before ordering.
Does IP67 mean I can use the tablet in the rain all day?
Yes for the enclosure. But IP does not cover operating temperature, sunlight readability, glove or wet-finger touch behaviour, or what happens when a hot device meets cold rain. Specify those separately, and check that port covers are seated every shift.
How do I check a supplier test report is genuine?
Ask for the full PDF report, not a certificate image. Check the accredited laboratory name (SGS, TUV, Intertek or equivalent), report number, standard and version cited, test dates, and that the tested sample matches your purchased configuration including ports, covers, battery and dock. Cross-check the report number with the issuing lab if the order is large.
Does customizing a tablet void its certifications?
It can. Adding a port, changing a gasket, enlarging a cut-out or fitting a third-party battery door changes the sealing path and can invalidate a previously passed IP test. Software-only changes do not affect ingress ratings. Any mechanical change should be followed by re-validation, which we cover in our customization process.
What is UN 38.3 and why does it matter?
UN 38.3 is the lithium battery transport test required for shipping devices by air or sea, documented in the UN Manual of Tests and Criteria section 38.3. Without it your shipment can be refused by carriers. Pair it with IEC 62133 for cell and pack safety. Ask your supplier for both, plus the material safety data sheet.
Do certifications expire?
Test reports do not expire, but they are tied to a specific product revision. A change of display supplier, battery cell or housing tooling makes the old report invalid for the new build. Market access rules also change: radio directive updates and national spectrum requirements can require re-testing. Ask for the report date and the hardware revision it covers.
